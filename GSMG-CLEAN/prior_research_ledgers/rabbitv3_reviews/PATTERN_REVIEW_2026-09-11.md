**How the attacks work, and where their conclusions fail.**

- The productive historical approach combines image-grid traversal, binary/ASCII conversion, cultural-reference solving, HTML inspection, SHA256 and OpenSSL AES-CBC. EBCDIC conversion plus Beaufort and a straddling checkerboard decode provide later readable texts. Public tools include CyberChef, dCode, ciphertools and Python notebooks. [Public solver record](https://github.com/puzzlehunt/gsmgio-5btc-puzzle), [local analysis](E:/rabbitv3/rabbit/analysis/README.md).
- The SHA256 of an answer is not automatically the AES key. The reproduced route uses the ASCII hexadecimal SHA256 digest as the password, then OpenSSL-compatible EVP derivation to obtain key and IV. The successful local controls use SHA256 inside EVP. Older MD5 defaults and PBKDF2 are separate hypotheses; OpenSSL changed its default digest in 1.1.0. [OpenSSL documentation](https://docs.openssl.org/1.1.1/man1/enc/).
- Labels such as matrixsumlist and lastwordsbeforearchichoice plausibly identify a computation and a text selector. Hashing those literal labels bypasses their possible function. The source does not prescribe a seven-label XOR recipe. Even the position of enter matters: its encoded form interrupts two 64-character Base64 lines. That supports a formatting role, without uniquely proving one. [Exact source segmentation](E:/rabbitv3/rabbit/evidence_review_2026-09-10/exact_text/salphaseion_segments.json).

It has one valid padding byte and approximately 40.2% printable bytes. The duplicated matrixsumlist hash cancels; the XOR is also insensitive to ordering. Its published output hash is a fingerprint of that computation, not a creator-supplied verifier. A CBC re-encryption round trip likewise verifies implementation, not intended plaintext. Binary plaintext is possible, so this is an unverified candidate rather than a mathematically impossible one.
